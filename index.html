Emergency guide

What counts as a plumbing emergency in San Antonio?

Not every plumbing problem needs a midnight phone call, but some do. A true emergency is any situation where water is causing or about to cause property damage, where there is a health or safety risk, or where you cannot control the problem yourself. In a city where many homes sit on concrete slab foundations and summer demand strains water systems, a small leak can become a large repair quickly.

Situations that commonly justify calling for after-hours help include:

  • A burst or leaking pipe that continues to release water after you shut off the fixture valve.
  • Sewage backing up into tubs, showers or floor drains, which is both unsanitary and damaging.
  • A failed water heater that is leaking heavily, making unusual noises, or producing no hot water in cold weather.
  • A toilet that overflows repeatedly and cannot be cleared with a plunger.
  • No water at all when the problem appears to be inside your home rather than a utility outage.
  • A suspected gas-related plumbing problem. If you smell gas, leave the building and contact your gas utility or 911 first.

Situations that can often wait until regular business hours include a slow drain, a dripping faucet, a running toilet, low pressure at a single fixture, or a minor leak you can contain with a bucket and a shutoff valve. Waiting can save money, since many providers charge more for nights, weekends and holidays. When you call, describe the situation honestly and ask whether the provider considers it urgent.

When to call us and when to call someone else

Plumbing Contractor Finder is a referral service, not an emergency responder. For fires, gas leaks, electrical hazards near water, or any danger to people, call 911 first. For a city water main break or a neighborhood-wide outage, contact the local water utility. For a problem inside your property, call us to ask about a referral to an independent plumbing provider.

First steps while you wait for help

  1. Shut off the water at the fixture’s valve, or at the main shutoff if you cannot find it.
  2. Turn off the water heater if pipes are leaking, and cut power to it if it is electric.
  3. Open a faucet at the lowest point of the house to relieve pressure and drain the line.
  4. Move rugs, electronics and furniture away from standing water. Stay clear of outlets and appliances if water is near them.
  5. Take photos or video of the damage for your records and any insurance claim.
  6. Keep your phone nearby and be ready to describe the problem and its location.

Know where your main shutoff valve is

The single most useful thing any homeowner or tenant can know is where the main water shutoff is. In many San Antonio homes it sits near the street in a ground-level meter box, though some properties also have a shutoff where the line enters the house, in a garage, utility closet or exterior wall. Locate yours now, make sure it turns freely, and tell everyone in the household. Renters should ask the landlord or property manager for the location and the correct procedure for reporting emergencies.

Common problems

Plumbing issues people in San Antonio ask about

The providers you may be referred to typically handle a wide range of residential and light commercial work. Services vary by provider, so always ask what a specific company handles. These are some of the issues callers most often describe.

Burst and leaking pipes

Pipes can fail from age, corrosion, shifting soil, high pressure or freezing. Water stains on walls or ceilings, the sound of running water when nothing is on, musty smells and unexpectedly high water bills are all warning signs. Because leaks often hide behind walls, under floors or in attics, a provider may need to inspect the line and locate the source before quoting a repair. Options can include patching a section, replacing a run of pipe or rerouting a line, depending on the material, access and condition.

Slab leaks

Many homes in the area are built on concrete slabs, with supply lines running beneath or within the foundation. A slab leak can develop when those lines corrode or are stressed by soil movement. Indicators include a water meter that keeps running when all fixtures are off, warm spots on the floor, the sound of water running under the house, damp carpet or flooring, and cracks that appear without explanation. Slab leaks are not always an overnight emergency, but they can cause significant damage, so ask a provider about leak detection and the available repair methods, which range from spot repairs to rerouting lines overhead.

Clogged drains and backups

A slow or blocked drain may result from grease, hair, soap buildup, food waste, flushed wipes or tree roots in an older sewer lateral. A single fixture that clogs is usually a local problem. When several drains slow down together or gurgle, the blockage may be in the main line. Mechanical clearing with a drain machine or camera inspection is usually safer for your pipes than repeated use of chemical cleaners, which can be hazardous and may damage older pipes.

Water heater problems

Tank-style water heaters commonly last roughly a decade or somewhat longer depending on use and water quality, and failures often happen without much notice. Signs include rusty water, popping or rumbling sounds from sediment, pooling water at the base, inconsistent temperature and no hot water. Tankless units can also develop scale and error codes. A provider can tell you whether repair or replacement makes more sense, and what size and fuel type suits your household.

Running or overflowing toilets

A toilet that runs constantly can waste a surprising amount of water. Worn flappers, fill valves and chains are common culprits and are usually inexpensive to repair. An overflowing toilet is different: turn the water off at the valve behind the toilet, avoid flushing again, and contact a provider if a plunger does not clear it.

Faucets, fixtures and low pressure

Dripping faucets and weak flow can be caused by worn cartridges, clogged aerators, mineral buildup or a failing pressure regulator. If pressure drops across the whole house, the cause may be a partially closed valve, a regulator issue or a leak. Describing which fixtures are affected helps a provider diagnose the problem faster.

Garbage disposals, dishwashers and appliance lines

Jammed disposals, leaking supply lines to washing machines and backed-up dishwashers are frequent calls. Braided supply hoses can fail over time and are a known cause of household flooding, so many plumbers recommend inspecting or replacing them periodically.

Gas lines and outdoor plumbing

Some plumbing providers also work on gas piping, outdoor spigots, sprinkler backflow devices and sewer line repairs. Not all do. If you need one of these services, say so at the start of the call.

Local conditions

Plumbing in San Antonio: what makes it different

San Antonio’s climate, soil and water supply create some plumbing challenges that homeowners in other regions may not face. Understanding them helps you describe problems accurately and ask better questions.

Hard water and mineral buildup

The San Antonio area draws heavily on the Edwards Aquifer, which is known for hard, mineral-rich water. Over time, calcium and magnesium can form scale inside water heaters, on fixtures, in aerators and in appliances. This can reduce efficiency, shorten equipment life, clog showerheads and contribute to sediment in tanks. Routine water heater flushing, descaling and, for some households, a water softener can help. Ask a provider what is appropriate for your equipment.

Expansive clay soil and shifting foundations

Soils in much of South Central Texas expand when wet and contract during dry spells. That movement can stress foundations and the plumbing lines in and beneath them. After long dry periods followed by heavy rain, some homeowners notice new leaks, cracks or changes in drainage. If you suspect soil movement is affecting your pipes, mention any foundation cracks or sticking doors when you speak to a provider.

Summer heat and high demand

Long hot summers mean heavy water use for cooling, landscaping and pools, which can reveal weaknesses in irrigation lines, hose bibs and pressure regulators. Exposed outdoor piping and water heaters in hot attics or garages also experience more thermal stress. Checking your water meter for unexplained movement is a simple way to catch hidden leaks early.

Freeze events

Hard freezes are infrequent in San Antonio, which means many homes have limited pipe insulation and exposed lines. When severe cold does arrive, burst pipes can follow, and plumbers become extremely busy. If a freeze is forecast, protect exposed pipes and spigots, open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, let faucets drip slowly, and know where your main shutoff is. If pipes have frozen, avoid open flames and thaw slowly. Expect longer waits for any provider during widespread freezes.

Older homes and aging infrastructure

Many established neighborhoods contain homes with older materials such as galvanized steel, cast iron drains, clay sewer laterals or polybutylene supply lines. These materials can corrode, crack or fail with age. If you live in an older home, ask a provider about the condition of your lines during any visit, and request that findings be explained clearly before agreeing to larger work.

Costs

What affects the cost of an emergency plumbing visit?

Plumbing Contractor Finder does not set or quote prices. Each independent provider sets its own rates and fees. We can’t tell you what a given job will cost, but we can explain the factors that commonly influence emergency pricing so you know what to ask.

  • Time of day. Nights, weekends and holidays often carry higher rates or a trip fee compared with weekday daytime service.
  • Diagnostic or service call fees. Some providers charge a flat fee to come out and assess the problem, which may or may not be applied toward the repair.
  • Flat rate versus hourly pricing. Some companies quote a fixed price per job, others bill labor by the hour plus parts. Ask which applies.
  • Parts and materials. Valves, fittings, water heaters and fixtures vary widely in price, and brand and quality affect cost.
  • Access and complexity. A pipe in an open crawl space is simpler than one behind a finished wall or under a slab.
  • Permits and code requirements. Some work, such as water heater replacement or gas line work, may require permits or inspections.
  • Cleanup and mitigation. Water extraction and drying are often handled by separate specialists, and may be a separate expense.

Questions to ask before approving work

  • What is your service call or trip fee, and does it apply to the repair?
  • Is the price a firm quote or an estimate, and what could change it?
  • Are after-hours or holiday surcharges included in that number?
  • How soon can a technician arrive, and how will I be contacted?
  • Will you explain the problem and the options before starting?
  • Is the work covered by a warranty, and what does it include?
  • What forms of payment do you accept?

Ask for the price in writing, or as a text or email, before work begins whenever possible. A reputable provider should be willing to explain charges clearly. Be cautious about anyone who refuses to give any estimate, pressures you to decide immediately, or asks for full payment in advance for work that has not started.

Choosing a provider

How to vet a plumber, especially in an emergency

Stress and urgency can make it tempting to accept the first person who answers. Taking a few minutes to verify credentials protects you from poor workmanship, unlicensed work and unexpected charges. Because the providers we connect callers with are independent, verification is your responsibility, and it is worth doing every time.

Check licensing

Texas requires plumbing work to be performed by or under the supervision of licensed professionals. The Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners regulates the trade and offers a public license lookup. Ask the provider for their license number, confirm it is current, and confirm it belongs to the company or individual you are speaking with. If a provider cannot or will not provide a license number, consider that a warning sign.

Confirm insurance

Ask whether the provider carries general liability insurance and workers’ compensation coverage, and request proof if the job is significant. Insurance matters if a mistake damages your property or a worker is injured on site.

Be wary of look-alike names and pressure tactics

Some online listings use names that resemble local businesses, and calls may be answered by a call center rather than the company that will send a technician. Ask who will actually perform the work and what name appears on their truck and invoice. Be skeptical of very low advertised prices that grow once someone arrives, and never feel obligated to approve work you do not understand.

Get the scope in plain language

Ask the provider to explain what is wrong, what they propose to do, and whether there are alternatives, such as a temporary fix now and a permanent repair later. For larger jobs, such as repiping or sewer line replacement, it is reasonable to request a second opinion during normal hours unless there is active damage.

Document everything

Keep invoices, estimates, license details, warranty terms and photos of the work. This record helps if you need to follow up on a problem or file an insurance claim.

Quick vetting checklist

  • License number provided and verified with the state
  • Insurance confirmed
  • Service area and arrival window confirmed
  • Fees and any after-hours charges explained
  • Written or texted estimate received
  • Warranty terms understood
Prevention

Reduce the odds of a middle-of-the-night plumbing call

No one can prevent every plumbing failure, but routine habits can lower the chances of an emergency and reduce damage when one occurs.

  • Watch your water bill and meter. A sudden increase or a meter that moves when everything is off often points to a hidden leak.
  • Test shutoff valves. Operate the main and fixture valves occasionally so they do not seize.
  • Flush your water heater. Sediment removal can help, particularly with hard water. Ask a professional if you are unsure how.
  • Inspect hoses. Check washing machine and dishwasher supply lines for bulges, cracks or rust, and replace them if they look worn.
  • Mind what goes down drains. Avoid pouring grease down the sink and do not flush wipes, even those labeled flushable.
  • Check pressure. Very high water pressure strains pipes and fixtures. A gauge on an outdoor spigot gives a quick reading.
  • Protect pipes before a freeze. Insulate exposed lines and cover outdoor spigots when cold weather is forecast.
  • Consider a leak detector. Small water sensors placed under sinks and near water heaters can alert you early.
  • Keep tree roots in check. Large trees near an older sewer line can invade it over time.
  • Schedule occasional inspections. An annual check by a licensed professional can catch problems before they become emergencies.

If you are a renter, report leaks and drainage issues to your landlord or property manager promptly and in writing. Delays can make damage worse and complicate responsibility for repairs.

What should I do first if a pipe bursts?

Shut off the main water valve, turn off the water heater, open a faucet to relieve pressure, move belongings away from water, and call for help. If there is any electrical or gas hazard, leave the area and call 911.

How do I verify a Texas plumber’s license?

Ask for the license number and check it with the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners. Also ask about current insurance before authorizing work.

Why do after-hours plumbers often cost more?

Many providers charge higher rates or trip fees for nights, weekends and holidays because of on-call staffing and scheduling costs. Ask for the fee structure before work begins.

How do I know if I have a slab leak?

Common signs include an unexplained rise in your water bill, a meter that runs when everything is off, warm spots on the floor, the sound of running water and damp flooring. A licensed provider can perform leak detection.

Should I use chemical drain cleaner for a clog?

A plunger is a safer first step. Chemical cleaners can be hazardous and may damage older pipes. Persistent or multi-fixture clogs should be assessed by a professional.
